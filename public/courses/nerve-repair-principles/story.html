Nerve Repair · Intermediate · ~35 min

Principles of Primary Nerve Repair

Learning objectives. By the end of this module you should be able to:

  • Choose the correct timing of repair for sharp, blunt and avulsive transections
  • Explain why a tension-free repair is the single most important technical principle
  • Describe epineurial versus group fascicular repair and the factors that predict outcome
Timing

When to repair

  • Sharp transection (knife, glass): repair within 48–72 hours when the wound is clean — ideally at first presentation. Early repair lets you identify fascicles before retraction and stump fibrosis.
  • Crush or avulsion mechanisms: the extent of nerve damage is not immediately demarcated. Tag the ends and delay definitive repair by roughly 2–3 weeks, when unhealthy nerve can be trimmed back to healthy, "pouting" fascicles [3,4].
  • The outer limit: muscle endplates degenerate over ~12–18 months of denervation. Motor repairs delayed beyond 3–6 months after injury lose ground progressively; distal motor targets are the most time-critical [1,3].
"Repair sharply-divided nerves early; repair bluntly-divided nerves once you can see healthy fascicles; do not let the calendar reach 6 months for motor nerves."
Tension

The enemy of regeneration

  • A repair under tension compromises intraneural blood flow and provokes fibrosis — regenerating axons stall or escape.
  • Mild tension can be absorbed by mobilising the nerve ends or modest joint positioning, but positioning that stretches the repair as the joint extends is a trap.
  • If the gap cannot be closed tension-free after trimming to healthy fascicles, graft — a tension-free graft beats a tense direct repair, the lesson consolidated by Millesi and confirmed by modern meta-analysis [1,3].
Technique

Epineurial vs group fascicular repair

  • Work under the operating microscope or loupes, with microsurgical instruments and 8-0 to 9-0 monofilament.
  • Trim both stumps with a fresh blade until normal fascicular architecture bulges from the cut face.
  • Align, then coapt: use surface landmarks — longitudinal vasa nervorum, fascicular size and topography — to avoid rotational mismatch.
  • Epineurial repair is the workhorse for most distal, single-level injuries. Group fascicular repair suits proximal mixed nerves where topography is well defined. Evidence does not clearly favour one overall; alignment quality and minimal suture burden matter more [4].
  • Use the fewest sutures that hold coaptation; fibrin glue can supplement, not replace, alignment.
Outcomes

What determines the result

  • The meta-analysis of median and ulnar nerve repairs by Ruijs et al. found recovery is better with younger age, distal injury level, shorter delay, and median (vs ulnar) nerve — but even modern microsurgical repair returns full function in a minority of adults [1].
  • Sensory re-education after repair measurably improves functional sensibility — rehabilitation is part of the operation, not an afterthought [2].
  • Set expectations early: recovery proceeds at ~1 mm/day and is usually useful rather than normal in adults.
Key points

Take-home messages

  1. Sharp → repair early; crush/avulsion → tag and repair at ~3 weeks.
  2. Never accept tension at the repair — graft instead.
  3. Trim to healthy fascicles, align meticulously, use minimal fine sutures.
  4. Prognosis rests on age, level, delay and nerve — counsel honestly.
