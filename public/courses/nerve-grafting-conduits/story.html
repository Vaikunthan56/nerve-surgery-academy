Nerve Grafting, Conduits and Allografts

Learning objectives. By the end of this module you should be able to:

  • State when a nerve gap requires a graft rather than a direct repair
  • Compare autograft, synthetic conduits, and processed nerve allograft
  • Apply a gap-length and nerve-type algorithm to choose between them
Autograft

The gold standard — and its price

  • Autologous nerve graft (classically sural nerve) provides living Schwann cells, basal lamina tubes, and neurotrophic support. It remains the benchmark against which everything else is judged [3,4].
  • Costs: a donor-site sensory deficit, possible painful neuroma, an extra scar, and limited graft material — a real constraint in long or multiple gaps.
  • Grafts are revascularised from the wound bed; very thick grafts risk central necrosis, so multiple cable grafts are preferred for large-calibre nerves.
A tension-free graft outperforms a tense direct repair — the gap itself is not the enemy; tension is.
Synthetic conduits

Tubes for short gaps

  • Commercial conduits (polyglycolic acid, collagen, polycaprolactone) provide an empty, protected channel across a gap — no donor morbidity, off the shelf.
  • The landmark randomised study by Weber et al. found PGA conduits at least equivalent to direct repair for digital nerve gaps, and superior for gaps >4 mm in that series [1].
  • Accepted envelope: pure sensory nerves, small diameter, gaps up to ~3 cm. Beyond that, empty tubes fail — there is no living scaffold to sustain regenerating axons.
Processed nerve allograft

Decellularised human nerve

  • Processed allograft is human nerve rendered acellular and non-immunogenic while preserving the endoneurial scaffold and laminin architecture — no donor-site morbidity and no harvest-time limit [2,3].
  • Early clinical series of allograft transplantation (with immunosuppression, in the pre-processing era) demonstrated that allograft tissue can support meaningful regeneration [3]; modern processed allografts achieve this without immunosuppression [2].
  • Comparative work shows acellular allograft supports regeneration approaching isograft/autograft performance in suitable models and clinical series — best established for sensory and mixed nerves with gaps up to ~5 cm [2].
  • Limitations: cost, variable evidence base for motor nerves and long gaps, and calibre matching.
Choosing between them

A working algorithm

SituationFirst choice
Tension-free coaptation possibleDirect repair
Sensory nerve, small calibre, gap < 3 cmConduit or allograft
Sensory/mixed nerve, gap ~3–5 cmProcessed allograft or autograft
Motor / large mixed nerve, or long gapAutograft (gold standard)
Proximal injury with long reinnervation distanceConsider nerve transfer instead

Local practice, cost and the evidence base evolve quickly — review the current literature before applying this dogmatically [2,4].

Key points

Take-home messages

  1. Autograft remains the reference standard; every alternative trades donor morbidity against regenerative quality.
  2. Conduits: short (<3 cm), small, sensory gaps only.
  3. Processed allograft fills the middle ground — scaffold without donor morbidity.
  4. When the target is too far for a graft to reach in time, think nerve transfer, not a longer graft.
