Nerve Transfers: Concepts and Common Donor Sets

Learning objectives. By the end of this module you should be able to:

  • Explain the biological logic of a nerve transfer and when it beats repair or grafting
  • Describe the principles of donor selection
  • Name the workhorse transfers for shoulder, elbow, and hand reanimation
The core concept

Converting a proximal injury into a distal one

  • A nerve transfer coapts an expendable, functioning donor fascicle or nerve to the denervated distal stump, close to the target muscle.
  • This sidesteps the fundamental problem of proximal injuries: at ~1 mm/day, axons from a repaired axillary or C5–6 lesion may reach the motor endplates only after they have degenerated.
  • Motor endplates become unreceptive after roughly 12–18 months of denervation — so transfers are planned early, and a delayed presentation is an argument for a transfer, not against [1,4].
Think of a transfer as "rewiring": you sacrifice a redundant function to buy a short, fast, reliable reinnervation distance for a critical one.
Donor selection principles

What makes a good donor

  • Expendable — sacrificing it leaves no clinically important deficit (ideally synergistic or redundant function).
  • Close to the target — minimises reinnervation time.
  • Size-matched, pure motor or pure sensory where possible — improves specificity of regeneration.
  • Direct coaptation without graft and without tension.
  • Donor must be outside the zone of injury — verify function clinically and electrophysiologically before sacrificing it.
Workhorse transfers

The donor sets every trainee should know

Function restoredTransfer
Shoulder abduction / external rotationSpinal accessory nerve → suprascapular nerve
Shoulder abductionTriceps branch (radial nerve) → axillary nerve
Elbow flexionOberlin: ulnar fascicle → musculocutaneous biceps branch; double fascicular (ulnar + median fascicles → biceps + brachialis branches) [3]
Wrist/finger extension (radial palsy)Median nerve branches (e.g. FCR/FDS) → posterior interosseous nerve
Intrinsic hand function in high ulnar injuryDistal anterior interosseous nerve → deep motor branch of ulnar nerve [2]

Meta-analysis of the shoulder/elbow literature supports transfers as reliable producers of useful (MRC ≥3–4) function when performed within months of injury [4].

Outcomes & rehabilitation

After the rewiring

  • Recovery begins within months because the coaptation is close to the target — the great advantage over proximal repair.
  • Patients must re-learn the function: activating the donor movement (e.g. ulnar-innervated grip) initially drives the recipient (elbow flexion); cortical plasticity usually makes this near-automatic over 1–2 years [1].
  • Results are best in younger patients, early surgery, and pure-motor donors to pure-motor recipients.
Key points

Take-home messages

  1. Transfers turn a long, slow, uncertain proximal reconstruction into a short, distal one.
  2. Time is nerve: refer suspected plexus and high nerve injuries early — the 12–18 month endplate clock cannot be reset.
  3. Know the workhorses: accessory→suprascapular, triceps→axillary, Oberlin/double fascicular for elbow flexion, AIN→ulnar motor.
  4. A transfer is only as good as the donor verification and the re-education that follows.
