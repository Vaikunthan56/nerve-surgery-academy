Compressive Neuropathies: Carpal & Cubital Tunnel

Learning objectives. By the end of this module you should be able to:

  • Diagnose carpal and cubital tunnel syndromes clinically and know when to add electrodiagnostics
  • Choose sensible conservative management and recognise when to escalate to surgery
  • Describe what the evidence says about open vs endoscopic release, and decompression vs transposition
Carpal tunnel syndrome

The commonest entrapment neuropathy

  • Population prevalence is around 3% in the classic JAMA study by Atroshi et al., with electrophysiological confirmation in about half of symptomatic hands [2].
  • Typical picture: night-time paraesthesia in the median digits, shaking the hand for relief, thenar weakness late. Provocative tests (Phalen, Durkan compression) and sensory mapping support — not make — the diagnosis [1].
  • NCS grade severity and provide a baseline before surgery; they matter most when the picture is atypical or surgery is contemplated.
  • Ask about drivers: pregnancy, diabetes, hypothyroidism, obesity, wrist arthritis [1].
CTS: management

Splint, inject, or release?

  • Mild–moderate, early: neutral wrist night splinting; a single corticosteroid injection gives good short-term relief and doubles as a diagnostic test [1].
  • Severe or persistent symptoms, thenar weakness/atrophy: surgical decompression — dividing the transverse carpal ligament — is definitive.
  • Open vs endoscopic: early RCTs suggested faster early recovery with endoscopic release, but extended follow-up shows the techniques converge — the JAMA long-term RCT found no clinically important difference at 5 years, with equivalent safety [3]. Technique choice is surgeon- and patient-dependent.
Counsel patients: symptom relief after release is usually prompt, but thenar muscle recovery and normalisation of sensation can take months in severe disease.
Cubital tunnel syndrome

The second commonest — and the contested operation

  • Ulnar neuropathy at the elbow: paraesthesia in the little/ring fingers, worse with elbow flexion; intrinsic weakness and clawing late. Grade severity (e.g. McGowan) clinically; NCS localise across the elbow.
  • Mild disease: activity modification and night extension splinting succeed in most patients with mild dysfunction [5].
  • Surgery: options are in situ decompression (open or endoscopic), anterior transposition (subcutaneous/submuscular), and medial epicondylectomy.
  • The meta-analysis by Macadam et al. found no significant difference in outcomes between simple decompression and transposition for idiopathic cubital tunnel syndrome — with a trend toward fewer complications with simple decompression [4]. Reserve transposition for nerve subluxation, severe disease, or a scarred/hostile bed [5].
Key points

Take-home messages

  1. CTS is a clinical diagnosis first; NCS grade severity and protect you pre-operatively.
  2. Splint/inject early CTS; release severe or refractory disease. Open and endoscopic release are equivalent long-term.
  3. Cubital tunnel: splint mild disease; for surgery, in situ decompression is as good as transposition for most idiopathic cases.
  4. In both, duration and severity of compression predict how much function returns after decompression.
