Classification of Nerve Injury: Seddon & Sunderland

Learning objectives. By the end of this module you should be able to:

  • Describe Seddon's three types of nerve injury and Sunderland's five grades, and how they map onto each other
  • Relate each grade to its anatomical substrate, clinical signs, and recovery potential
  • Explain how injury grade drives the timing of surgical intervention
The Seddon classification (1943)

Three types of nerve injury

Writing from wartime experience, Seddon defined three injury types that remain the everyday clinical shorthand [1]:

  • Neurapraxia — focal myelin injury causing a conduction block; axons and connective tissue intact. No Wallerian degeneration. Full recovery, typically within days to ~12 weeks.
  • Axonotmesis — axons and myelin disrupted, but endoneurium, perineurium and epineurium preserved. Wallerian degeneration occurs distally; recovery depends on axonal regrowth at roughly 1 mm/day.
  • Neurotmesis — complete disruption of the entire nerve. No spontaneous recovery; surgery is required.
Clinical correlate: neurapraxia has no Tinel sign and no denervation on EMG; axonotmesis shows an advancing Tinel sign as axons regenerate.
The Sunderland classification (1951)

Five grades, defined by which layers are lost

GradeStructures disruptedSpontaneous recovery
IMyelin only (= neurapraxia)Complete; days–weeks
IIAxon + myelin; endoneurium intactGood; axons regrow within their tubes
III+ endoneurium; perineurium intactVariable; intrafascicular fibrosis misdirects axons
IV+ perineurium; only epineurium intactPoor — neuroma-in-continuity; surgery usually needed
VComplete transection (= neurotmesis)None without repair

Mackinnon and Dellon later added a grade VI: a mixed injury in which different fascicles in the same nerve sustain different grades — arguably the commonest pattern in penetrating trauma [3,4].

Why the grade matters

From classification to clinical decision

  • Grades I–II are managed expectantly: recovery is predicted by the distance from lesion to target at ~1 mm/day.
  • If a closed injury shows no clinical or electrical recovery by ~3 months, the lesion is behaving like grade III–V and surgical exploration is indicated.
  • Grade IV–V lesions cannot recover spontaneously: they need resection of scar and repair or grafting.
  • MR neurography can now image fascicular disruption and neuroma-in-continuity, mapping the Seddon/Sunderland grade onto surgical planning non-invasively [3].
A non-advancing Tinel sign at the level of injury, with absent motor unit potentials at 3 months, is the classic picture of a lesion that will not recover without surgery.
Key points

Take-home messages

  1. Seddon gives you three clinically useful categories; Sunderland gives you the anatomical detail that predicts recovery.
  2. The single most important distinction for the junior surgeon: is the endoneurial tube intact? If yes, regeneration can proceed; if no, recovery fails.
  3. Recovery rate ≈ 1 mm/day guides how long to wait — and when to stop waiting.
  4. Mixed (grade VI) injuries are common; treat each fascicular population on its merits.
