Diagnostics · Intermediate · ~30 min

Electrodiagnostic Studies for the Junior Surgeon

Learning objectives. By the end of this module you should be able to:

  • Explain what nerve conduction studies (NCS) and needle EMG actually measure
  • Time electrodiagnostic studies correctly after nerve injury — and say why early studies mislead
  • Use serial studies to decide between continued observation and surgery
What the tests measure

NCS and EMG in plain terms

Nerve conduction studies

  • CMAP (compound muscle action potential): motor axons. Amplitude reflects the number of functioning motor axons; latency/conduction velocity reflect myelin.
  • SNAP (sensory nerve action potential): sensory axons distal to the dorsal root ganglion.

Needle EMG

  • Fibrillations and positive sharp waves = denervated muscle fibres.
  • Motor unit action potentials (MUAPs) — their absence, recruitment pattern, and later "nascent" (small, polyphasic) reinnervation potentials tell you whether axons are reaching muscle.
A preserved SNAP with a paralysed, sensory-loss limb suggests a lesion proximal to the dorsal root ganglion — e.g. root avulsion in brachial plexus injury [1,4].
Timing is everything

Why "too early" means "useless"

  • Wallerian degeneration takes about 72 hours to complete: in the first days after transection the distal stump still conducts, so NCS can look deceptively normal [2].
  • Fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves take 2–3 weeks to appear in denervated muscle (longer proximally).
  • The first informative window is therefore around 3–4 weeks after injury.
  • Reinnervation is tracked serially: nascent MUAPs on EMG often precede clinical recovery by weeks and can justify continued observation.
Practical rule: a study done in the first week answers "is the nerve in continuity electrically?", not "will it recover?". Recovery questions need the 3–4 week baseline and a ~3 month repeat.
Interpretation

What electrodiagnostics can and cannot tell you

  • Can: localise the lesion; distinguish demyelinating conduction block (neurapraxia) from axonal loss; detect early reinnervation; separate preganglionic from postganglionic plexus lesions.
  • Cannot: reliably distinguish Sunderland grades III, IV and V from each other — all show complete axonal loss. That distinction is clinical and, increasingly, radiological (MR neurography / ultrasound).
  • An absent CMAP with no reinnervation at 3–4 months after a closed injury is a strong argument for exploration and repair [1,2].
Key points

Take-home messages

  1. Order NCS/EMG at 3–4 weeks, not in the first days, and repeat at ~3 months if recovery is in doubt.
  2. Read the report actively: CMAP/SNAP amplitudes, fibrillations, MUAP recruitment — not just the conclusion line.
  3. Electrodiagnostics complements — never replaces — the advancing Tinel sign and serial clinical examination.
  4. The decision to operate is made on the trend: no clinical and no electrical recovery by 3–4 months = surgery.
